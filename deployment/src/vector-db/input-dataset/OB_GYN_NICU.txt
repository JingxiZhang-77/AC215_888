Hospital-Wide Core Policy
Title: Management of Disruptive and Threatening Behavior by Patients, Families, and Visitors
1. Purpose
To establish a standardized, coordinated process for identifying, responding to, documenting, and reviewing disruptive and threatening behavior by patients, families, and visitors across the hospital. The policy aims to protect staff, patients, and visitors; maintain a safe care environment; and ensure appropriate escalation, communication, and follow-up.
2. Scope
This policy applies to:
All clinical and non-clinical staff (employees, contractors, volunteers, trainees).
All patients, families, care partners, and visitors in inpatient, outpatient, perioperative, emergency, diagnostic, and procedural areas.
All forms of disruptive or threatening behavior occurring on hospital property or via hospital-managed communication channels (phone, portal, telehealth).
3. Definitions
Disruptive behavior
Any action by a patient, family member, or visitor that interferes with the ability of staff to safely provide care, disrupts operations, or creates a hostile or unsafe environment. Examples include: repeated yelling or verbal abuse, refusal to follow safety rules, harassment, or intimidation.
Threatening behavior
Behavior that reasonably raises concern for imminent or potential harm to self or others, including verbal threats (â€œI will hurt youâ€), physical gestures (raising a fist), stalking, brandishing objects as weapons, or explicit threats to property.
Acute threat
A situation where there is an immediate or imminent risk of serious harm to staff, patients, or visitors (e.g., physical assault, active violence, weapon display, credible threats of immediate harm). These require urgent activation of security/rapid response and may involve law enforcement.
Non-acute disruptive behavior
Behavior that is disruptive, abusive, or escalating but does not pose an immediate risk of serious physical harm. Examples: repeated verbal abuse, refusal to comply with staff instructions, boundary violations, low-level intimidation.
Support person / care partner
A family member, friend, or designated individual who accompanies the patient and participates in their care or provides emotional support.
Behavioral alert / safety flag
An EHR or internal system notation used to indicate a patient or associated individual with a history of significant disruptive or threatening behavior requiring special precautions.
Care agreement / behavior contract
A written, time-limited agreement between the care team and patient/family outlining expected behaviors, boundaries, and consequences for non-adherence (e.g., restricted visitors, requirement for security presence).
Escalation pathway
The ordered sequence of individuals/services to be notified as behavior escalates (e.g., primary nurse â†’ charge nurse â†’ attending â†’ security â†’ administrative supervisor â†’ law enforcement).
Interdisciplinary team meeting
A structured meeting involving representatives from relevant disciplines (e.g., nursing, physician, social work, security, risk management, patient relations) to review complex or recurrent cases and agree on a unified behavior management plan.

5. Risk Levels & Examples
Low-risk disruptive behavior
Examples: raised voice, mild verbal frustration, refusal to answer questions, non-compliance with minor processes (e.g., refusing a gown).
Response: staff-level de-escalation, boundary setting, brief documentation as needed.
Moderate-risk disruptive behavior
Examples: repeated yelling or insults, targeted verbal harassment, refusal to comply with safety instructions, frequent boundary violations, threatening body language without explicit threats.
Response: de-escalation plus involvement of charge nurse/attending; consider security standby; behavior plans and care agreements; clear documentation.
High-risk / acute threatening behavior
Examples: physical aggression (hitting, pushing, throwing objects), credible verbal threats of harm, weapon display, stalking, barricading, credible threats of self-harm in a way that endangers others.
Response: immediate activation of security/rapid response or â€œcodeâ€ per hospital protocol, law enforcement as appropriate, possible limitation or termination of visitation, urgent interdisciplinary review.

7. Roles and Responsibilities
Frontline clinical staff (nurses, physicians, techs, therapists)
Identify and classify disruptive behavior.
Use de-escalation techniques and set behavioral limits.
Notify charge nurse/attending and security per escalation pathway.
Document behaviors, interventions, and outcomes.
Charge nurse / unit leader
Oversee immediate response and ensure staff safety measures.
Coordinate with physicians, social work, security, and supervisors.
Determine need for care agreements or visitor restrictions.
Ensure EHR alerts and handoff communication are updated.
Attending physician / advanced practice provider
Lead clinical decision-making regarding treatment and disposition.
Participate in communication with the patient/family about behavior and consequences.
Author or co-sign care agreements and behavior-related orders.
Security services
Respond promptly to calls for assistance.
Assist with scene safety, patient/visitor removal from the area if necessary.
Liaise with law enforcement when indicated.
Document security incident reports per protocol.
Social work / behavioral health
Support de-escalation and crisis intervention.
Address underlying social, psychiatric, or substance-related contributors.
Participate in care planning and meetings.
Administrative supervisor / manager-on-call
Support units during acute incidents and complex cases.
Authorize visitor restrictions, location changes, or other operational measures.
Facilitate interdisciplinary case reviews.
Risk Management / Patient Relations (as needed)
Advise on complex or recurrent cases.
Support communication with patients/families on rights and responsibilities.
8. Step-by-Step Procedures
8.1 Non-Acute Disruptive Behavior
Recognition
Staff identify behavior that is disruptive but not imminently dangerous.
Initial staff-level response
Use calm tone, non-threatening body language.
Acknowledge concerns (â€œI hear that you are frustrated.â€).
Clearly state expectations and limits.
Offer problem-solving options
Clarify delays, provide approximate timelines, or offer alternative solutions.
Set boundaries
Inform the individual that abusive or threatening behavior is not acceptable and will lead to escalation.
Notify charge nurse/attending if behavior persists or escalates.
Consider security standby for repeat or escalating behaviors.
Document the incident, including specific behaviors, interventions, and response.
Update handoffs to include behavior and current plan.
8.2 Acute Threatening Behavior
Immediate assessment of safety
Staff remove themselves and others from immediate danger when possible.
Activate emergency response
Call designated security code or emergency number, and/or local law enforcement as per hospital protocol.
Do not physically intervene unless trained and absolutely necessary to prevent imminent serious harm.
Secure the area
Restrict access, remove bystanders where safe.
Clinical stabilization
Once safe, provide necessary medical and psychiatric evaluation.
Communication and notification
Notify charge nurse, attending physician, administrative supervisor, and security leadership.
Post-incident actions
Determine need for visitor restriction, behavior contract, or transfer to higher security unit.
Enter or update behavioral alerts in EHR.
Complete incident reports and security reports.
Conduct staff debrief.
8.3 Care Agreements and Restrictions
Indications
Recurrent disruptive behavior, repeated boundary violations, or history of threats without adequate adherence to previous plans.
Development
Interdisciplinary team drafts agreement specifying acceptable behaviors, prohibited behaviors, communication expectations, and consequences.
Communication
Agreement is reviewed with patient and relevant family/support persons; questions addressed.
Documentation
Signed copy scanned/uploaded into EHR; summary placed in problem list or notes.
Enforcement
Staff consistently enforce consequences for non-adherence (e.g., end of visit, removal from waiting room, restriction of support persons).
Review
Periodic review to determine if agreement can be modified or discontinued.

2.3 OB/GYN & NICU Department Policy
Title: OB/GYN & NICU Policy for Managing Disruptive and Threatening Behavior
Purpose
To protect mothers, infants, and staff by incorporating behavior management into labor & delivery, postpartum, gynecologic services, and NICU, while specifically reducing procedure count failures, dose and order verification errors, and missed identification of high-risk conditions in the context of disruptive behavior.
Scope
Applies to L&D, postpartum, gynecology inpatient units, OB triage, and NICU.
Department-Specific Risks & Behaviors
Intense family conflict at bedside during labor interfering with count procedures and medication checks.
Aggressive partners or family members pressuring staff during emergent cesarean sections, increasing risk for count errors and dosing errors.
Disruptive behavior overshadowing early signs of high-risk conditions (e.g., preeclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage).
Operational Procedures (OB/GYN & NICU Focus)
Procedure Counts (Sponges, Instruments, Sharps)
If disruptive behavior occurs during counts (e.g., shouting, physical interference):
The circulating nurse announces â€œcount pauseâ€ and stops all non-essential tasks.
Security or additional staff support is requested to manage the disruptive individual.
Counts are restarted from the last confirmed complete step and documented.
Medication and Order Verification
For oxytocin, magnesium sulfate, antihypertensives, and neonatal medications:
Double-checks are mandatory when the environment has been disrupted in the previous 30 minutes.
Orders are verified directly in the EHR; no verbal-only orders accepted when disruptive behavior is present.
Any interruption during medication preparation triggers a repeat verification.
Identification of High-Risk Conditions
Disruptive situations (e.g., partner yelling, multiple visitors arguing) must not delay timed assessments (BP checks, fetal monitoring interpretation, NICU vitals).
If assessment is delayed due to behavioral safety concerns, nurse:
Documents delay and reason.
Notifies charge nurse/attending and requests security support.
High-risk conditions (e.g., severe-range BP, heavy bleeding) are escalated using standard protocols regardless of ongoing behavioral conflict.
NICU Visitor Management
Visitors demonstrating disruptive or threatening behavior are immediately escorted out by security; visitation privileges may be temporarily restricted.
Any restrictions are clearly documented and communicated at shift change.
Scenarios (OB/GYN & NICU)
During an emergent C-section, a partner becomes verbally aggressive about anesthesia; staff call security, complete counts with security present, and document the event.
In NICU, a parentâ€™s repeated yelling causes the nurse to delay medication preparation; policy requires the nurse to stop, reset, verify medication with a second nurse, administer, and document delay.
